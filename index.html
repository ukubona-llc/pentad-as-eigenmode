## The Cabernet Analogy: What is a Commodity?

Your analogy is on the right track, but a true tech commodity is even more fungible (exchangeable) than wine. While a Californian and Chilean Cabernet are both red wines, they still have distinct terroirs and flavor profiles that buyers care about.

A true commodity is completely interchangeable and bought almost entirely on price and raw specifications—think crude oil, winter wheat, or alternating current electricity. In the tech world, things like standard DRAM memory chips or basic x86 processors often trend toward becoming commodities.

## 14 Years Ago: Fighting the Hardware Commodity

A decade ago, Huang's primary goal was preventing NVIDIA's GPUs from becoming a commodity. If a GPU is just a standard chip, hardware manufacturers are easily swapped out for whoever makes them cheapest, and profit margins collapse.

To fight this, NVIDIA didn't just build hardware; they built CUDA, a proprietary programming platform. By doing so, they ensured their chips weren't just exchangeable pieces of silicon, but the necessary foundation of a locked-in ecosystem.

## Today: Manufacturing a New Commodity

Recently, his rhetoric has flipped because the computing paradigm has shifted. He now frequently describes NVIDIA's massive data centers not as computing servers, but as "AI factories."

Look at the modern computing landscape. Systems capture noisy user behavior—often with significant error or loss in measurement—and feed it into massive training pipelines using stochastic gradient descent (SGD). What that SGD process produces is an engine capable of generating a brand new commodity: intelligence, quantified as tokens.

Just like a power plant generates generic electrons, an AI factory generates tokens. Companies buy these standard, fungible tokens, wrap them in a frictionless UI/UX, and use them to lock users into their own software ecosystem. By positioning NVIDIA as the factory that produces this intelligence, Huang has moved from fighting hardware commoditization to owning the very means of producing the software world's most valuable new commodity.

## Cabernet vs. Tap Water

In this context, your Cabernet analogy is actually too generous. Wine implies some level of craft and distinction, where buyers still care about the origin and the subtle differences (Chile vs. California).

When Jensen uses "commodity" here, he means something completely undifferentiated and strictly utilitarian—like tap water, winter wheat, or generic AA batteries.

The early web portals thought search was just a basic, low-value utility. They assumed any search engine was as good as another, so it wasn't worth their time to build it. They bought it on the cheap. By treating search as an exchangeable commodity and outsourcing it, they accidentally paved the way for Google—who realized search wasn't a commodity at all, but the actual key to the internet.

When Jensen talks about "commodities" today (like AI tokens), he's using the same definition: a raw, standardized utility that powers everything else. But this time, instead of outsourcing it, he's building the factory that generates it.
